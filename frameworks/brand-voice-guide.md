# Brand voice guide

> **Credit:** Diane Wiredu, [Lion Words](https://www.lionwords.com/). Her verbal identity guide structure and her approach to voice, taught in the CXL AI Native Marketer cohort, Marketing Brain workshop. The credit lives here, in the framework. The guide this produces carries the brand's name only: no Lion Words or Diane Wiredu name, logo, link or credit in any output.

**Brand** is your business's personality. **Brand voice** is how you communicate that personality through language, in every word you publish: website copy, sales materials, emails, social media, customer comms. Done well, it works like meeting someone you click with: instant connection, and you can't quite say why.

"Warm, human, friendly" tells nobody how to write, and it tells Claude even less. A voice guide works when every rule in it changes something you can see on the page, so the voice **can be identified and replicated across the business**: by a new hire, an agency, or Claude. Exercise 3 fills this into [[wiki/brand/voice-guide|voice-guide]] and [[wiki/brand/vocabulary|vocabulary]].

## Before the voice: the core four

The voice speaks **to the ICP** (exercise 1) **using the messaging** (exercise 2). The guide names both at the top of section 1 and proves it in the appendix by saying the hub's messages to the rich avatar.

Before you can create messaging that is sticky, memorable and compelling, you need the basics. Prospects should be able to answer four questions about the product. Most messaging problems come down to these four not being **clear, consistent, or agreed internally**. Get them locked in, then get creative. They come from the positioning and messaging hub (exercise 2).

| | Question | Check |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarity** | **1. What is it?** | **Category:** is it clear what type of product or service this is? **Use case:** is it clear what prospects can do with it? |
| **Relevance** | **2. Who is it for?** | **Target customer:** is it clear who this is for: company type, job type, department or role, persona? |
| **Value** | **3. Why should I care?** | **Problem solved:** is it clear what problems it solves? **Benefits:** is what I gain clear, specific and tangible? |
| **Advantage** | **4. What sets you apart?** | **Alternatives:** what do you replace? **Unique attributes:** what can you do that the alternatives can't? |

If the hub leaves any of the four blank, the voice guide says so. The voice can't fix them.

## Voice has three pillars

Aside from vision, values and personality, voice comes down to three things you can see on the page. Nail the combination and the voice is recognisable.

| Pillar | What it controls |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | Which words and phrases you use, and which you never use. Lives in [[wiki/brand/vocabulary]]. |
| **Cadence** | The rhythm and sentence length: how sentences follow each other, where the short punches land, how long they run. |
| **Tone** | How the voice flexes by context: a launch, a support reply, an apology. |

A trait only counts if it changes one of these three.

## The output format

The output is a **verbal identity document**, laid out exactly like this, top to bottom. Use the brand's name wherever the template says COMPANY.

```
# Verbal Identity: Brand Voice & Writing Guidelines

> 💡 **BRAND VOICE:** *How we communicate our brand personality, values, purpose, and messaging through language and voice.* **Use case:** *This applies to all the words we publish. Website copy, sales materials, emails, social media, customer comms, etc.*

1. Our voice in a nutshell
2. Brand voice traits (matrix)
3. Tone profile
4. Writing principles
5. Language and terminology
   - Phrases to use
   - Phrases to avoid
6. Branded language: the [PRODUCT] / [signature element]
   - Overview: visual and verbal consistency
   - Practical application

---

## 1. Our voice in a nutshell
...
```

- The contents list is a numbered list of **links** to each section and sub-section, with the sub-sections indented under 5 and 6.
- A horizontal rule separates the contents from section 1.
- Six numbered sections, in this order, with these headings. Nothing else sits between them.
- The working material (core four check, proof before and after, sources, open (inferred) tags) goes **after** section 6, under a second horizontal rule and an `## Appendix` heading, so the guide reads as a finished document first.

## What goes in each section

### 1. Our voice in a nutshell
- **Written for / Saying,** two lines at the top: who the voice talks to (the rich avatar from [[wiki/brand/icp|icp]]) and what it says (the owned key message from [[wiki/brand/positioning-messaging|positioning-messaging]]), each cited.
- **Persona line,** on its own: "COMPANY's voice personified is a **'[Role] with a [quality]'**", for example "a 'Trusted Coach with a playful streak'". A role your buyer would trust, and a quality that shows up in the writing.
- **How it reads:** one prose paragraph, three to five sentences, not bullets. Start "Our copy reads like..." and describe what a reader experiences: what the writer does, how they treat the reader, and what they never do. End on the core personality in one line. For example: "Our copy reads like an expert who pulls up a chair, explains complex topics clearly without talking down to you, slips in the occasional light joke, and helps you feel confident and in control. Our core personality: professional competence with human warmth. We take the work seriously, but not ourselves too seriously."
- **Voice blend:** "**Voice blend:** our audience hears the following layers:" then a short list, one layer per line, each a quality and what it sounds like. The blend is where the brand's personality meets the audience's: it should sound like the brand, and like someone the buyer would want to talk to.

### 2. Brand voice traits (matrix)
Three to five traits. Every trait names the pillar it changes. If you can't fill that column, cut the trait.

| Trait | What it means | Do | Don't | Changes (vocabulary / cadence / tone) | Example from a real sample |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 3. Tone profile
How the voice flexes by context, while staying the same voice. One row per context the brand actually writes in.

| Context | Tone | What shifts | Example |
|---|---|---|---|

### 4. Writing principles
- **Cadence and sentence length:** measured from real samples: average and longest sentence, how paragraphs open, fragments and questions. Rules about rhythm, not word caps: use as many words as necessary, but not one more.
- **Always / never:** specific enough to pass or fail a draft.
- **This, not that:** 8 to 12 near-miss pairs from real sentences. Nuance, not extremes: "We cut your reporting time" vs "We streamline your reporting workflow", not vs "Synergize your paradigm". Each pair names what changed: the pillar, or the fluff quadrant.
- **Fluff check:** the fluff matrix below, with one real off-brand line per fluff quadrant, its fix, and the fluff-free target.

### 5. Language and terminology
Two sub-sections, in the guide itself, so the guide reads complete on its own:
- **Phrases to use:** the owned words and the allowed jargon that matter most, as a short table (phrase, what it means or why we use it).
- **Phrases to avoid:** the banned buzzwords, each with what to write instead, as a short table.

Open with the rule in one line: jargon your buyers use stays, buzzwords go. Close with a link to [[wiki/brand/vocabulary|vocabulary]], which holds the full lists, the evidence for each, and customer words vs ours.

### 6. Branded language: the [PRODUCT] / [signature element]
Name the product and the signature element in the heading itself, for example "the Acme Deals / deal-drop element".
- **Overview: visual and verbal consistency.** The product, feature and method names, owned terms, and the **signature element**: the recurring device that makes the brand recognisable (a mascot, a named concept, a recurring phrase), and how it stays consistent visually and verbally.
- **Practical application.** Where it appears, where it never appears, and how to write it (spelling, capitalisation, with or without "the").

### Appendix, after the six sections
- **Core four check:** can a prospect answer each of the four from the hub? Answered, and where.
- **Brain test: the hub, in this voice, to the ICP.** A hero block for the rich avatar: headline from the owned key message, subhead from the customer-facing value proposition, three bullets from the pillar statements. Each line lifted from the hub and changed only where the guide or the vocabulary demands, with the rule named. A hub line the voice can't say is a finding for the hub or the guide.
- **Proof: before and after.** One off-brand sample rewritten using only this guide and the vocabulary, and what changed.
- **Sources** and **open (inferred) tags.**

## The fluff matrix

Most buyers feel fluff the second they read it; they just can't say why they're scrolling past. Fluff is measurable, to a point: it is **a lack of substance**, and substance comes down to two axes.

| Axis | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|
| **Content: how specific** | **Vague.** Generic claims and benefits, little detail, no proof. | **Specific.** Detailed claims backed by real numbers or customer results. |
| **Delivery: how natural** | **Formulaic.** Clichéd phrasing, buzzwords, overused playbook language. | **Natural.** Sounds how buyers actually talk. Easy to understand. |

That gives four quadrants, three of them fluff. Diane's own example lines show one claim moving through all four:

| | Vague | Specific |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | **Empty charm** (fluff). Sounds warm and relatable, but there's no substance behind it. Hooks you for a second, then you forget it. *"Help your teams respond faster, smarter, and more efficiently."* | **Fluff-free zone** (the goal). Clear and credible, in the language your buyers actually use. Helpful, with real substance. *"Cut ticket response time by 40% with automated support triage."* |
| **Formulaic** | **Corporate blandspeak** (fluff). Says a lot, means nothing. Empty promises dressed up in clichés and big words that try to sound important. *"Empower your teams to unlock growth through innovative support solutions."* | **Jargon jungle** (fluff). Technically accurate, but exhausting to read. Substance buried under buzzwords and dense, over-polished phrasing. *"Leverage our AI-powered orchestration layer to boost response times by 40%."* |

### Why fluff costs you
- **It's open to misinterpretation.** Fluff buries the real value. Does "streamline your sales" mean automating tasks, or cutting steps and doing it faster? Leave prospects guessing and you've lost control of your product narrative.
- **It blends into category noise.** Make the same generic claims as everyone else ("transform", "revolutionise", "grow your revenue") and you become "just another [blank]". Buyers have heard those promises so often they're white noise.
- **It doesn't land with buyers.** Nobody has ever said "I can't wait to unlock the power of our CRM." Mirroring real customer language works better than trying to sound "professional".

### The two levers
1. **Get specific** about the problem you solve and the solution you offer.
2. **Use customer language** to describe buyer needs and frustrations.

Fluff is rarely intentional. It's bad habits mixed with a little fear of saying what you really mean. The fix is simpler than it looks.

### How to audit a draft
Place every claim on the matrix. For each one outside the fluff-free zone, ask two questions: **"How, exactly?"** (moves it right, toward specific) and **"Would a buyer say this out loud?"** (moves it up, toward natural). Use this lens to critique drafts in all three exercises, not only this one.

## Jargon is not the enemy

People conflate jargon with buzzwords all the time. They're not the same thing.

- **Jargon** is insider language: words that need specialised knowledge to understand. When you know your buyers well, it makes a connection and shows you get their world. Selling to developers? You'd better say DevOps, lifecycle, sprints, deploy, commit, debug, rollback.
- **Buzzwords** are loaded words that drifted into popular culture, everyday business, and now every B2B website: leverage, seamless, solutions, innovative, unleash, empower, scalable, AI-powered, streamline, supercharge. They have several meanings or none, and leave the reader asking "what do they actually do?" They carry a little emotional value (they make you feel something smooth for a second), which is why they are everywhere. They fall apart the moment you ask **"how?"**

Is a buzzword *always* wrong? No. But assume your brand is not the exception. And keeping copy simple must not mean dumbing it down: strip out real jargon and a technical buyer hears an outsider. Record which words are which in [[wiki/brand/vocabulary|vocabulary]].
